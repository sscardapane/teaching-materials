\section{Normalization}
\subsection{Normalization layers}

% Written by Claude (2026-10-20). Fixes the 2024 slide: column index in the
% statistics, and "Hessian of X" (the Hessian is that of the least-squares loss).
\begin{frame}{Normalizing the inputs}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    Given a dataset $\vc{X}\sim(N,D)$, we usually standardize each column
    (feature) to zero mean and unit variance:
    \[
        \idx{\vc{X}'}{i,j} = \frac{\idx{\vc{X}}{i,j} - \mu_j}{\sigma_j} \,, \qquad
        \mu_j = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \idx{\vc{X}}{i,j} \,, \quad
        \sigma_j^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \left(\idx{\vc{X}}{i,j} - \mu_j\right)^2 .
    \]
    For least squares, the Hessian of the loss is $\frac{2}{N}\vc{X}^\top\vc{X}$.
    After standardization its diagonal is constant, so the problem is usually
    better \textbf{conditioned}: remember the elongated valley in the
    preliminaries.

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        Idea: do the same inside the network, on the output of every layer.
        The difficulty is that these outputs change at every step of training.
    \end{tcolorbox}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Batch normalization}
    \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}

    Ideally, we would standardize each unit with its mean and variance over the
    whole training set. But these change at every step, and recomputing them
    would need a full pass over the data (with gradients through all of it).
    Instead, we estimate them on the mini-batch. For outputs $\vc{Z}\sim(B,D)$:
    \[
        \widetilde{\mu}_j = \frac{1}{B}\sum_b \idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} \,, \quad
        \widetilde{\sigma}_j^2 = \frac{1}{B}\sum_b \left(\idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} - \widetilde{\mu}_j\right)^2 , \quad
        \idx{\text{BN}(\vc{Z})}{b,j} = \gamma_j \frac{\idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} - \widetilde{\mu}_j}{\sqrt{\widetilde{\sigma}_j^2 + \varepsilon}} + \beta_j \,.
    \]
    A \myalert{batch normalization} (BN) layer standardizes each column, then
    learns a new mean $\beta_j$ and scale $\gamma_j$ for it ($2D$ trainable
    parameters). It usually goes between a linear layer and its activation,
    whose bias becomes redundant.

    \SlideSource{Ioffe and Szegedy (2015), \textit{Batch normalization: accelerating deep network training by reducing internal covariate shift}, ICML.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Batch normalization in CNNs}
    \setlength{\parskip}{3pt}

    After a convolutional layer, the outputs on a mini-batch are
    $\vc{Z}\sim(B,H,W,C)$. Each channel is the same filter applied at every
    position, so we treat every position of every image as one more sample of
    that channel:
    \[
        \widetilde{\mu}_c = \frac{1}{BHW}\sum_{b,i,j} \idx{\vc{Z}}{b,i,j,c} \,, \qquad
        \widetilde{\sigma}_c^2 = \frac{1}{BHW}\sum_{b,i,j} \left(\idx{\vc{Z}}{b,i,j,c} - \widetilde{\mu}_c\right)^2 .
    \]
    Each channel is then standardized and rescaled as before, with one pair
    $\gamma_c, \beta_c$ per channel ($2C$ parameters).

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsblue]
        A channel plays the role of a feature: the mean is taken over the
        mini-batch \emph{and} over all spatial positions.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    In PyTorch, this is \texttt{nn.BatchNorm2d(C)} (with channels first).

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Batch normalization at inference}
    \setlength{\parskip}{3pt}

    During training, each output depends on the rest of its mini-batch. At
    inference we want a deterministic prediction, so
    we need fixed values $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j^2$.

    We could compute them on the training set after training. In practice, BN
    keeps a running average of the mini-batch statistics, at almost no cost:
    \[
        \mu_j \leftarrow (1-m)\,\mu_j + m\,\widetilde{\mu}_j \qquad (m = 0.1 \text{ in PyTorch})
    \]
    and similarly for $\sigma_j^2$, with the unbiased estimate
    $\frac{B}{B-1}\widetilde{\sigma}_j^2$. They replace the estimates at inference.

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsorange]
        In PyTorch, \texttt{model.train()} and \texttt{model.eval()} switch
        between the two modes: forgetting \texttt{model.eval()} is a classic bug.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{Wu and Johnson (2021), \textit{Rethinking ``batch'' in BatchNorm}.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How the running average works}

    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}%
            \small\setlength{\parskip}{5pt}%
            \RaggedRight\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}%
            Unrolling the update over past mini-batches:
            \[
                \mu_j = m \sum_{k \ge 0} (1-m)^k \, \widetilde{\mu}_j^{(t-k)} \,.
            \]
            Older estimates count exponentially less: an
            \textbf{exponential moving average}. With $m=0.1$, the last 10
            mini-batches make up 65\% of the value, the last 50 over 99\%.

            Example: starting from $\mu_j = 0$, if every mini-batch gives
            $\widetilde{\mu}_j = 1$, the running mean is $0.1$ after one step,
            $0.65$ after 10 and $0.99$ after 50.
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
            \centering
            \begin{tikzpicture}
                \begin{axis}[
                        width=\linewidth, height=0.55\textheight,
                        ybar, bar width=2.2pt, ymin=0, ymax=0.11,
                        xmin=-1, xmax=30, x dir=reverse,
                        xlabel={Mini-batches ago ($k$)}, ylabel={Weight},
                        label style={font=\small}, tick label style={font=\scriptsize},
                        yticklabel style={/pgf/number format/fixed, /pgf/number format/precision=2},
                        axis lines=left, axis line style={drawgray},

                    \addplot[fill=drawblue, draw=none, domain=0:29, samples=30] {0.1*0.9^x};
                \end{axis}
            \end{tikzpicture}

            {\scriptsize Weight $m(1-m)^k$ of each past estimate ($m=0.1$).}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fusing batch normalization at inference}
    \setlength{\parskip}{4pt}\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{4pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{4pt}

    BN usually sits between a linear layer and its activation:
    $\vc{h} = \phi\left(\text{BN}(\vc{W}\vc{x} + \vc{b})\right)$. At inference,
    $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j^2$ are fixed, so BN is just an elementwise affine map:
    \[
        \text{BN}(\vc{z}) = \vc{a} \odot \vc{z} + \vc{c} \,, \qquad
        a_j = \frac{\gamma_j}{\sqrt{\sigma_j^2 + \varepsilon}} \,, \quad
        c_j = \beta_j - a_j \mu_j \,.
    \]
    Two affine maps in a row are one affine map, so we can fold BN into the layer:
    \[
        \text{BN}(\vc{W}\vc{x} + \vc{b}) = \vc{W}'\vc{x} + \vc{b}' \,, \qquad
        \vc{W}' = \text{diag}(\vc{a})\,\vc{W} \,, \quad
        \vc{b}' = \vc{a} \odot \vc{b} + \vc{c} \,.
    \]

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        After training, BN costs nothing at inference. The same works for a
        convolution, one output channel at a time.
    \end{tcolorbox}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Layer normalization}
    \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}

    Tying each example to its mini-batch is costly: noisy estimates with small
    mini-batches, a poor fit for variable-length sequences or many devices, and
    different behavior in training and inference. \myalert{Layer normalization}
    (LN) computes the statistics of each example over its own features:
    \[
        \widetilde{\mu}_b = \frac{1}{D}\sum_j \idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} \,, \quad
        \widetilde{\sigma}_b^2 = \frac{1}{D}\sum_j \left(\idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} - \widetilde{\mu}_b\right)^2 , \quad
        \idx{\text{LN}(\vc{Z})}{b,j} = \gamma_j \frac{\idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} - \widetilde{\mu}_b}{\sqrt{\widetilde{\sigma}_b^2 + \varepsilon}} + \beta_j \,.
    \]

    \begin{center}
        \small
        \begin{tabular}{lll}
               & \textbf{Statistics over}            & \textbf{Parameters} $\gamma, \beta$ \\
            \hline
            BN & each feature, across the mini-batch & one pair per feature                \\
            LN & each example, across its features   & one pair per feature                \\
        \end{tabular}
    \end{center}

    Nothing depends on other examples: the same computation in training and
    inference, even with $B=1$. LN is the standard in transformers.

    \SlideSource{Ba et al. (2016), \textit{Layer normalization}.}

\end{frame}

        \end{column}

        \end{column}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RMSNorm: dropping the mean}
    \setlength{\parskip}{4pt}\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{4pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{4pt}

    Most recent LLMs skip the mean: \myalert{RMSNorm}$(\vc{z}) = \vc{\gamma} \odot \vc{z} / \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{D}\sum_j z_j^2 + \varepsilon}$.

    Why can we drop the mean? Centering is a linear map:
    $\vc{z} - \text{mean}(\vc{z})\,\vc{1} = \vc{P}\vc{z}$, with
    $\vc{P} = \vc{I} - \frac{1}{D}\vc{1}\vc{1}^\top$. When LN follows a linear
    layer, $\vc{P}$ can be folded into it:
    \[
        \text{LN}(\vc{W}\vc{x} + \vc{b}) = \text{RMSNorm}(\vc{P}\vc{W}\vc{x} + \vc{P}\vc{b}) + \vc{\beta} \,.
    \]
    So centering adds nothing that the previous layer cannot learn. The only
    nonlinear part of LN is the division by the norm, which RMSNorm keeps.

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        In practice, RMSNorm matches LN in accuracy and reduces running time by
        7--64\% across models.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{Zhang and Sennrich (2019), \textit{Root mean square layer normalization}, NeurIPS.}

\end{frame}

\subsection{Why normalization works}

\begin{frame}{Why does BN help?}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    The original explanation was \textbf{internal covariate shift}: each layer
    sees inputs whose distribution keeps changing as the previous layers train,
    and BN stabilizes them.

    Later experiments challenged this explanation: injecting noise after BN to shift the
    distributions again barely hurts training. A better-supported view is that
    BN makes the loss landscape \textbf{smoother}, so the gradient stays
    predictive over longer steps.

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        In practice, the most robust effect is that normalized networks train
        stably with much \textbf{larger learning rates}.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{Santurkar et al. (2018), \textit{How does batch normalization help optimization?}, NeurIPS;
        Bjorck et al. (2018), \textit{Understanding batch normalization}, NeurIPS;
        Lipton and Steinhardt (2018), \textit{Troubling trends in machine learning scholarship}.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Normalized layers ignore the scale of their weights}
    \small
    \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}
    \setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{5pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{5pt}

    A unit produces $\vc{z}=\vc{X}\vc{w}$, followed by BN. If we scale its
    weights by $a>0$, the batch mean and standard deviation scale by $a$:
    \[
        \mu(a\vc{z})=a\mu(\vc{z}) \,, \qquad
        \sigma(a\vc{z})=a\sigma(\vc{z}) \,.
    \]
    We neglect $\varepsilon$ and keep $\gamma,\beta$ fixed. The scale cancels:
    \[
        \text{BN}(a\vc{z})
        = \gamma\,\frac{a\vc{z}-a\mu(\vc{z})\vc{1}}{a\sigma(\vc{z})}
          +\beta\vc{1}
        = \text{BN}(\vc{z}) \,.
    \]

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsblue]
        The predictions, and hence the data loss, do not change:
        $\mathcal{L}(a\vc{w})=\mathcal{L}(\vc{w})$.
        Only the \textbf{direction} of these weights matters to the function.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    For LN, the same holds only when we scale the \emph{whole} preceding
    layer, weights and bias together: we come back to this at the end.

    \SlideSource{van Laarhoven (2017), \textit{L2 regularization versus batch and weight normalization}.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Scale invariance determines the gradient}

    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}%
            \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}%
            \RaggedRight\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}%
            \small
            Take $\vc{g}(\vc{w})=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\vc{w})$ and differentiate $\mathcal{L}(a\vc{w})=\mathcal{L}(\vc{w})$:

            \textbf{1. In the scalar $a$, at $a=1$:}
            \[
                \left.\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm da}\mathcal{L}(a\vc{w})\right|_{a=1}
                =\vc{w}^{\top}\vc{g}(\vc{w})=0 \,.
            \]
            The gradient is \textbf{orthogonal to $\vc{w}$}, tangent to its sphere.

            \textbf{2. In the vector $\vc{w}$, with $a$ fixed:}
            \[
                a\,\vc{g}(a\vc{w})=\vc{g}(\vc{w})
                \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
                \vc{g}(a\vc{w})=\frac{\vc{g}(\vc{w})}{a} \,.
            \]
            The chain rule supplies the factor $a$ on the left.
            Longer weights get \textbf{smaller gradients}.
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.36\textwidth}
            \centering
            \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.86]
                \foreach \ang in {5,25,...,165} {
                    \draw[drawgray!35] (0,0) -- (\ang:2.8);
                }
                \draw[drawgray] (0,0) circle (2);
                \draw[-{Stealth[length=2mm]}, thick, drawblue] (0,0) -- (40:2)
                    node[midway, below right, font=\scriptsize] {$\vc{w}$};
                \draw[-{Stealth[length=2mm]}, thick, draworange] (40:2) -- ++(130:1.1)
                    node[above left, font=\scriptsize] {$-\eta\vc{g}$};
                \draw[dashed, drawgray] (0,0) -- ($(40:2) + (130:1.1)$);
                \fill (0,0) circle (1pt);
            \end{tikzpicture}

            {\scriptsize The loss is constant along each gray ray, so the
            gradient has no component along $\vc{w}$.\par}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \SlideSource{van Laarhoven (2017), \textit{L2 regularization versus batch and weight normalization}.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The weight norm sets the effective learning rate}
    \small
    \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}
    \setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{5pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{5pt}

    We separate length and direction: $\vc{w}=r\vc{u}$, with
    $r=\lVert\vc{w}\rVert$ and $\lVert\vc{u}\rVert=1$.
    The previous identity gives $\vc{g}(r\vc{u})=\vc{g}(\vc{u})/r$.
    A gradient-descent step is therefore
    \[
        \vc{w}^{+}=r\vc{u}-\frac{\eta}{r}\vc{g}(\vc{u})
        =r\left(\vc{u}-\frac{\eta}{r^2}\vc{g}(\vc{u})\right) \,.
    \]
    We divide by its new norm to recover the direction:
    \[
        \vc{u}^{+}
        =\frac{\vc{u}-\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}\vc{g}(\vc{u})}
        {\lVert\vc{u}-\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}\vc{g}(\vc{u})\rVert}
        \,,\qquad
        {\color{drawblue}\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\eta}{r^2}} \,.
    \]

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        For a small step, $\vc{u}^{+}-\vc{u}\approx
        -\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}\vc{g}(\vc{u})$.
        Doubling $r$ leaves the function unchanged, but divides this change
        in direction by four.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{van Laarhoven (2017), \textit{L2 regularization versus batch and weight normalization}. Gradient descent, without momentum.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Weight decay as a learning rate}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    Since $\vc{w}\perp\vc{g}$, plain gradient steps only grow $r$, so
    $\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}=\eta/r^2$ keeps falling. \textbf{Weight decay},
    $\vc{w}^{+}=(1-\eta\lambda)\vc{w}-\eta\vc{g}(\vc{w})$, pulls the other way:
    \[
        (r^{+})^2=
        \underbrace{(1-\eta\lambda)^2r^2}_{\text{decay shrinks the norm}}
        +\underbrace{\eta^2\lVert\vc{g}(\vc{w})\rVert^2}_{\text{gradient step grows it}}
    \]
    When the two balance, each step turns $\vc{u}$ by about $\sqrt{2\eta\lambda}$.

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsorange]
        Here decay does not change the function: it sets the
        \textbf{effective learning rate}, through $\eta\lambda$.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{van Laarhoven (2017), \textit{L2 regularization versus batch and weight normalization};
        Li et al.\ (2020), \textit{Reconciling modern deep learning with traditional optimization analyses: the intrinsic learning rate}, NeurIPS.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why normalize across features?}
    \setlength{\parskip}{3pt}

    Standardizing each column suits inputs measured in different units. LN
    averages across the features of one example: why is this reasonable in
    hidden layers?
    \begin{itemize}\small
        \item \textbf{Hidden units are interchangeable.} They come from the same
              kind of linear map and start identically distributed (same reasoning as He initialization).
        \item \textbf{LN fixes the length of the representation}, as we see
              in the next slide.
    \end{itemize}

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsorange]
        This assumes units contribute in similar ways. In CNNs, units near the
        image border behave differently: there, BN still works better.
    \end{tcolorbox}

\begin{frame}{Understanding LN}

    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}%
            \small\setlength{\parskip}{4pt}%
            \RaggedRight\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}%

            \textbf{1. Centering.} Given $\vc{z} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, centering with $\vc{z}_c = \vc{z} - \mu\vc{1}$ makes the vector orthogonal to $\vc{1}$ ($\vc{z}^\top_c\vc{1} = 0$) and is equivalent to projecting onto the subspace perpendicular to $\vc{1}$ (grey plane on the right).

\begin{frame}{Residual connections}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    In a \myalert{residual network}, we add a \myalert{skip connection} around
    each block $f$ (for example, two convolutions):

    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
            \[
                r(\vc{x}) = f(\vc{x}) {\color{darkred} + \vc{x}} \,.
            \]
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
            \centering
            \scalebox{0.85}{\input{../../shared/assets/images/lecture_4/residual.tex}}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsblue]
        $r$ is a \textbf{residual block}: $f$ is the residual branch, $\vc{x}$
        the skip path. With $f = 0$ the block is the identity, so every block
        only learns a \textbf{change} to its input.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    If $\vc{x}$ and $f(\vc{x})$ have different shapes, we map $\vc{x}$ with a
    linear layer (a $1\times1$ convolution for images). The same block is in
    every modern architecture, transformers included.

    \SlideSource{He et al. (2016), \textit{Deep residual learning for image recognition}, CVPR.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Residuals without normalization}
    \small
    \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}

    With He initialization and no BN, a branch keeps the scale of its input,
    so every block roughly doubles the variance:
    \[
        \text{Var}(\vc{h}_{l+1}) \approx \text{Var}(\vc{h}_l) + \text{Var}(f(\vc{h}_l)) \approx 2\,\text{Var}(\vc{h}_l) \,.
    \]
    After 27 blocks, a factor of $2^{27} \approx 10^8$: our 56-layer network
    never trains. Two fixes keep each branch small:

    \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
            \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsblue,courseheight={resfix}{}]
                \textbf{BN in the branch}

                The branch has unit variance while the skip path grows, so
                block $l$ adds only about $1/l$ of the variance.
            \end{tcolorbox}
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
            \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen,courseheight={resfix}{}]
                \textbf{Start from the identity}

                Zero the last layer of each branch, or use
                $\vc{x} + \alpha f(\vc{x})$ with a learned $\alpha = 0$
                at the start.
            \end{tcolorbox}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    Zeroing the last convolution is enough: 88.1\% without BN ($\eta = 0.02$),
    91.4\% with BN ($\eta = 0.1$).

    \SlideSource{De and Smith (2020), \textit{Batch normalization biases residual blocks towards the identity function in deep networks}, NeurIPS;
        Goyal et al. (2017); Zhang et al. (2019), Fixup; Bachlechner et al. (2021), ReZero.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Neural ODEs}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    With $T$ residual blocks, the hidden state evolves as
    \[
        \vc{h}_t = \vc{h}_{t-1} + f(\vc{h}_{t-1}) \,, \qquad t = 1, \ldots, T \,,
    \]
    with $\vc{h}_0 = \vc{x}$. In the limit $T \rightarrow \infty$, each block
    only makes an infinitesimal step. We cannot have infinitely many layers,
    but we can condition a single block on $t$:
    \[
        \frac{\mathrm d\vc{h}(t)}{\mathrm dt} = f(\vc{h}(t), t) \,.
    \]
    An ODE solver computes the output, and we call these models
    \myalert{neural ODEs}.

    \SlideSource{Chen et al. (2018), \textit{Neural ordinary differential equations}, NeurIPS.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Can deep networks overfit?}
    \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsblue]
        A model \textbf{overfits} when its performance keeps improving on the
        training set while it gets worse on a separate validation set.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[height=0.48\textheight]{images/deeper/random_labels.png}
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}%
            \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}%
            \RaggedRight\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}%
            Deep networks are strangely resilient to classical overfitting,
            yet they can \textbf{memorize} anything: trained long enough, a CNN
            fits CIFAR-10 perfectly even with random labels (red) or random
            pixels.

            So the architecture alone does not explain why they generalize
            with real labels.
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \SlideSource{Zhang et al. (2017), \textit{Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization}, ICLR, Fig.~1a.}

\end{frame}

% 2024 "Finding a stopping point" and "Early stopping" in one slide.
\begin{frame}{Early stopping}
    \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}

    In optimization, we would stop when the loss stops changing,
    $\lvert\mathcal{L}(\vc{w}_t)-\mathcal{L}(\vc{w}_{t-1})\rvert\le\varepsilon$.
    This is rarely done: large networks are limited by compute, small ones by
    overfitting. With \myalert{early stopping} we watch a validation set instead:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item we keep a portion of the data as the \myalert{validation set};
        \item after each epoch, we compute the validation loss (or accuracy);
        \item if it has not improved for a number of epochs (the
              \myalert{patience}), we stop and keep the best checkpoint.
    \end{enumerate}

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        Early stopping is common with small datasets, and shows the difference
        between pure optimization and learning.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    LLMs see most of their data once: the compute budget decides when to stop.

\end{frame}

\subsection{Penalties and parameterizations}

% 2024 "Improving training with regularization" and "Weight decay": the L2
% penalty and AdamW are now in the MLP deck, the effective learning rate in
% the normalization section; this slide connects the two.
\begin{frame}{Weight decay, again}
    \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}

    Large weights make the network less \textit{smooth}: small changes in the
    input give sharp changes in the output. We met two views of weight decay,
    $\vc{w} \leftarrow (1-\eta\lambda)\vc{w} - \eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\vc{w})$:

    \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
            \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsblue,courseheight={wd}{}]
                \textbf{A penalty} (MLP lecture)

                $\lambda\lVert\vc{w}\rVert^2$ prefers smaller weights: with
                $\lambda=0$ nothing changes, with $\lambda$ too large all
                weights go to zero.
            \end{tcolorbox}
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
            \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsorange,courseheight={wd}{}]
                \textbf{A learning rate} (normalization)

                Before BN or LN, the function ignores $\lVert\vc{w}\rVert$:
                decay only sets $\eta/\lVert\vc{w}\rVert^2$.
            \end{tcolorbox}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    In a modern network both are at work. With Adam, we use \textbf{AdamW},
    which applies the decay directly instead of through the gradient. Biases
    and normalization parameters are usually not decayed.

    \SlideSource{Loshchilov and Hutter (2019), \textit{Decoupled weight decay regularization}, ICLR.}

\end{frame}

% 2024 "Other forms of regularization" and "Graphical depiction of LASSO"
% (figure from The Elements of Statistical Learning, Fig. 3.11).
\begin{frame}{Other forms of regularization}

    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}%
            \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}%
            \RaggedRight\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}%
            Many other penalties exist. Replacing the squared norm with the sum
            of absolute values,
            \[
                \min_{\vc{w}} \; \mathcal{L}(\vc{w}) + {\color{darkred} C \sum_j \lvert w_j \rvert} \,,
            \]
            leads to \textbf{sparse} solutions, with many weights exactly zero.

            Penalizing whole groups of weights (all the outgoing weights of a
            unit) removes entire units, and so shrinks the network.
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/deeper/lasso.png}

            {\scriptsize The corners of the $\ell_1$ ball (left) lie on the
                axes, where some weights are zero.\par}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \SlideSource{Hastie et al., \textit{The Elements of Statistical Learning}, Fig.~3.11;
        Scardapane et al. (2017), \textit{Group sparse regularization for deep neural networks}, Neurocomputing.}

\end{frame}

% 2024 "On weight reparameterization": same content, with the link to weight
% decay made explicit.
\begin{frame}{On weight reparameterization}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    The $\ell_1$ norm $\lVert\vc{w}\rVert_1 = \sum_j \lvert w_j \rvert$ is hard
    to optimize with SGD, because it is not differentiable at $0$. We can
    \textit{reparameterize} $\vc{w} = \vc{a} \odot \vc{b}$ and use weight decay
    on the new vectors:
    \[
        \mathcal{L}(\vc{w}) + C\lVert\vc{w}\rVert_1
        \quad\rightarrow\quad
        \mathcal{L}(\vc{a} \odot \vc{b}) + \frac{C}{2}\left(\lVert\vc{a}\rVert^2 + \lVert\vc{b}\rVert^2\right) .
    \]
    The minima are the same (since $\tfrac12(a^2+b^2) \ge \lvert ab \rvert$),
    but the new problem is smooth and much easier for SGD.

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        How we \textbf{parameterize} the same function can change training a
        lot, as we saw with normalization.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{Ziyin and Wang (2022), \textit{Sparsity by redundancy: solving $L_1$ with a simple reparametrization}, arXiv:2210.01212.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mixing examples: MixUp and CutMix}
    \setlength{\parskip}{4pt}

    We can also augment by \textbf{combining} two examples
    $(\vc{x}_1, \vc{y}_1)$ and $(\vc{x}_2, \vc{y}_2)$, with one-hot labels.
    \myalert{MixUp} takes a convex combination with a random $\lambda \in [0,1]$:
    \[
        \vc{x} = \lambda\vc{x}_1 + (1-\lambda)\vc{x}_2 \,, \qquad
        \vc{y} = \lambda\vc{y}_1 + (1-\lambda)\vc{y}_2 \,.
    \]
    MixUp images look unnatural. \myalert{CutMix} pastes a random patch of
    $\vc{x}_2$ instead, \mbox{$\vc{x} = (1-\vc{M}) \odot \vc{x}_1 + \vc{M} \odot \vc{x}_2$},
    with $\lambda$ the fraction of the image left to $\vc{x}_1$.

        \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{images/deeper/mixing.png}

        \begin{tabular*}{0.6\textwidth}{@{\extracolsep{\fill}}cccc@{}}

            \makebox[0.12\textwidth]{MixUp: 70\% cat} & \makebox[0.12\textwidth]{CutMix: 75\% cat}
        \end{tabular*}
    \end{center}

\end{frame}

\subsection{Dropout}

% 2024 "Data augmentation revisited" and "Visualization of dropout" (book
% figure, Alice's Adventures in a Differentiable Wonderland) in one slide.
\begin{frame}{From noisy inputs to noisy layers}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    Why does augmentation help? Small \myalert{perturbations} of the input
    make the network more robust: training with noise is provably a form of
    regularization.

    \myalert{Dropout} brings the same idea inside the network: we perturb the
    hidden layers by randomly \textit{dropping} some of their units.

    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{images/deeper/dropout_masks.pdf}
    \end{center}

    Each mask selects a sub-network: we train many of them, with shared weights.

    \SlideSource{Bishop (1995), \textit{Training with noise is equivalent to Tikhonov regularization}, Neural Computation.}

\end{frame}

% 2024 "Dropout regularization": shapes in the course convention.
\begin{frame}{Dropout regularization}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    Take the output $\vc{H}\sim(B,D)$ of a layer with $D$ units on a
    mini-batch of $B$ examples. During training, dropout replaces it with
    \[
        \widetilde{\vc{H}} = \vc{H} \odot \vc{M} \,,
    \]
    where every entry of $\vc{M}\sim(B,D)$ is drawn independently: $0$ with
    probability $p$, $1$ with probability $1-p$.

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsblue]
        If $\idx{\vc{M}}{b,j} = 0$, unit $j$ is dropped for example $b$. Every
        example and every step sees a different sub-network.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    Dropout can also be applied to the input of the network.

    \SlideSource{Srivastava et al. (2014), \textit{Dropout: a simple way to prevent neural networks from overfitting}, JMLR.}

\end{frame}

% 2024 "Dropout at inference time".
\begin{frame}{Dropout at inference time}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    At inference, we want a deterministic output. The simplest choice is the
    expected value of the dropout \textit{layer}, which we can compute in
    closed form:
    \[
        \mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\vc{H}}] = p \cdot \vc{0} + (1-p)\,\vc{H} = (1-p)\,\vc{H} \,.
    \]
    It is more convenient to rescale during training instead
    (\myalert{inverted dropout}):
    \[
        \widetilde{\vc{H}} = \frac{\vc{H} \odot \vc{M}}{1-p}
        \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
        \mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\vc{H}}] = \vc{H} \,.
    \]

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        With inverted dropout, at inference we simply remove the layer. This is
        what \texttt{nn.Dropout(p)} does, switched by \texttt{model.eval()}.
    \end{tcolorbox}

\end{frame}

% 2024 "Montecarlo dropout".
\begin{frame}{Monte Carlo dropout}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    With dropout, the output $f(\vc{x}; \vc{M})$ is a random variable that
    depends on the mask. A more principled prediction is the expected value
    over the masks of the whole \textit{network}:
    \[
        \hat{\vc{y}} = \mathbb{E}_{p(\vc{M})}\left[f(\vc{x}; \vc{M})\right]
        \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N f(\vc{x}; \vc{M}_i) \,, \qquad \vc{M}_i \sim p(\vc{M}) \,.
    \]
    We keep dropout active at inference and average $N$ forward passes:
    \myalert{Monte Carlo dropout}.

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        The spread of the $N$ predictions gives an estimate of the model's
        \textbf{uncertainty}, at the cost of $N$ forward passes.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{Gal and Ghahramani (2016), \textit{Dropout as a Bayesian approximation: representing model uncertainty in deep learning}, ICML.}

\end{frame}

% 2024 "Variants of dropout", plus stochastic depth (new).
\begin{frame}{Variants of dropout}
    \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item In a convolutional layer, dropout removes single values of single
              channels, which neighboring pixels easily replace.
              \myalert{Spatial dropout} drops entire channels; \myalert{cutout}
              drops a patch of the image.
        \item \myalert{DropConnect} drops single weights instead of units:
              $\widetilde{\vc{h}} = \phi\left((\vc{W} \odot \vc{M})\,\vc{x}\right)$.
        \item \myalert{Stochastic depth} drops entire residual branches,
              $r(\vc{x}) = \vc{x} + m\,f(\vc{x})$ with a random $m \in \{0,1\}$.
              A residual network barely notices a missing block, as we saw.
    \end{itemize}

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsorange]
        Plain dropout is less common in CNNs, and more common in
        transformers and recurrent networks.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{Tompson et al. (2015), CVPR; DeVries and Taylor (2017); Wan et al. (2013), ICML; Huang et al. (2016), \textit{Deep networks with stochastic depth}, ECCV.}

\end{frame}

    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]

        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}%
            \small\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}%
            \RaggedRight\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}%
            Dropout can be switched on or off during training.
            \myalert{Early dropout} reduces the variance of SGD in the first
            epochs and helps models that \textit{underfit}. \myalert{Late
                dropout} fights overfitting.
