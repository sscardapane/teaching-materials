\begin{frame}{The rest of the lecture}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    A good initialization (such as He's) only fixes the scale of the
    activations at the first step. Nothing keeps it there during training, and
    the gradient must cross every layer to reach the first ones.

    We look at three possible solutions to improve training at scale:

    \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.32\textwidth}
            \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsblue,courseheight={roadmap}{}]

\section{Normalization}
\subsection{Normalization layers}

% Written by Claude (2026-10-20). Fixes the 2024 slide: column index in the
% statistics, and "Hessian of X" (the Hessian is that of the least-squares loss).
\begin{frame}{Normalizing the inputs}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    Given a dataset $\vc{X}\sim(N,D)$, we usually standardize each column
    (feature) to zero mean and unit variance:
    \[
        \idx{\vc{X}'}{i,j} = \frac{\idx{\vc{X}}{i,j} - \mu_j}{\sigma_j} \,, \qquad
        \mu_j = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \idx{\vc{X}}{i,j} \,, \quad
        \sigma_j^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \left(\idx{\vc{X}}{i,j} - \mu_j\right)^2 .
    \]
    For least squares, the Hessian of the loss is $\frac{2}{N}\vc{X}^\top\vc{X}$.
    After standardization its diagonal is constant, so the problem is usually
    better \textbf{conditioned}: remember the elongated valley in the
    preliminaries.

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        Idea: do the same inside the network, on the output of every layer.
        The difficulty is that these outputs change at every step of training.
    \end{tcolorbox}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Batch normalization}
    \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}

    Ideally, we would standardize each unit with its mean and variance over the
    whole training set. But these change at every step, and recomputing them
    would need a full pass over the data (with gradients through all of it).
    Instead, we estimate them on the mini-batch. For outputs $\vc{Z}\sim(B,D)$:
    \[
        \widetilde{\mu}_j = \frac{1}{B}\sum_b \idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} \,, \quad
        \widetilde{\sigma}_j^2 = \frac{1}{B}\sum_b \left(\idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} - \widetilde{\mu}_j\right)^2 , \quad
        \idx{\text{BN}(\vc{Z})}{b,j} = \gamma_j \frac{\idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} - \widetilde{\mu}_j}{\sqrt{\widetilde{\sigma}_j^2 + \varepsilon}} + \beta_j \,.
    \]
    A \myalert{batch normalization} (BN) layer standardizes each column, then
    learns a new mean $\beta_j$ and scale $\gamma_j$ for it ($2D$ trainable
    parameters). It usually goes between a linear layer and its activation,
    whose bias becomes redundant.

    \SlideSource{Ioffe and Szegedy (2015), \textit{Batch normalization: accelerating deep network training by reducing internal covariate shift}, ICML.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Batch normalization at inference}
    \setlength{\parskip}{3pt}

    During training, each output depends on the rest of its mini-batch. At
    inference we want a deterministic prediction, so
    we need fixed values $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j^2$.

    We could compute them on the training set after training. In practice, BN
    keeps a running average of the mini-batch statistics, at almost no cost:
    \[
        \mu_j \leftarrow (1-m)\,\mu_j + m\,\widetilde{\mu}_j \qquad (m = 0.1 \text{ in PyTorch})
    \]
    and similarly for $\sigma_j^2$, with the unbiased estimate
    $\frac{B}{B-1}\widetilde{\sigma}_j^2$. They replace the estimates at inference.

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsorange]
        In PyTorch, \texttt{model.train()} and \texttt{model.eval()} switch
        between the two modes: forgetting \texttt{model.eval()} is a classic bug.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{Wu and Johnson (2021), \textit{Rethinking ``batch'' in BatchNorm}.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How the running average works}

    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}%
            \small\setlength{\parskip}{5pt}%
            \RaggedRight\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}%
            Unrolling the update over past mini-batches:
            \[
                \mu_j = m \sum_{k \ge 0} (1-m)^k \, \widetilde{\mu}_j^{(t-k)} \,.
            \]
            Older estimates count exponentially less: an
            \textbf{exponential moving average}. With $m=0.1$, the last 10
            mini-batches make up 65\% of the value, the last 50 over 99\%.

            Example: starting from $\mu_j = 0$, if every mini-batch gives
            $\widetilde{\mu}_j = 1$, the running mean is $0.1$ after one step,
            $0.65$ after 10 and $0.99$ after 50.
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
            \centering
            \begin{tikzpicture}
                \begin{axis}[
                        width=\linewidth, height=0.55\textheight,
                        ybar, bar width=2.2pt, ymin=0, ymax=0.11,
                        xmin=-1, xmax=30, x dir=reverse,
                        xlabel={Mini-batches ago ($k$)}, ylabel={Weight},
                        label style={font=\small}, tick label style={font=\scriptsize},
                        yticklabel style={/pgf/number format/fixed, /pgf/number format/precision=2},
                        axis lines=left, axis line style={drawgray},
                    ]
                    \addplot[fill=drawblue, draw=none, domain=0:29, samples=30] {0.1*0.9^x};
                \end{axis}
            \end{tikzpicture}

            {\scriptsize Weight $m(1-m)^k$ of each past estimate ($m=0.1$).}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fusing batch normalization at inference}
    \setlength{\parskip}{4pt}\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{4pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{4pt}

    BN usually sits between a linear layer and its activation:
    $\vc{h} = \phi\left(\text{BN}(\vc{W}\vc{x} + \vc{b})\right)$. At inference,
    $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j^2$ are fixed, so BN is just an elementwise affine map:
    \[
        \text{BN}(\vc{z}) = \vc{a} \odot \vc{z} + \vc{c} \,, \qquad
        a_j = \frac{\gamma_j}{\sqrt{\sigma_j^2 + \varepsilon}} \,, \quad
        c_j = \beta_j - a_j \mu_j \,.
    \]
    Two affine maps in a row are one affine map, so we can fold BN into the layer:
    \[
        \text{BN}(\vc{W}\vc{x} + \vc{b}) = \vc{W}'\vc{x} + \vc{b}' \,, \qquad
        \vc{W}' = \text{diag}(\vc{a})\,\vc{W} \,, \quad
        \vc{b}' = \vc{a} \odot \vc{b} + \vc{c} \,.
    \]

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        After training, BN costs nothing at inference. The same works for a
        convolution, one output channel at a time.
    \end{tcolorbox}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Batch normalization in CNNs}
    \setlength{\parskip}{3pt}

    After a convolutional layer, the outputs on a mini-batch are
    $\vc{Z}\sim(B,H,W,C)$. Each channel is the same filter applied at every
    position, so we treat every position of every image as one more sample of
    that channel:
    \[
        \widetilde{\mu}_c = \frac{1}{BHW}\sum_{b,i,j} \idx{\vc{Z}}{b,i,j,c} \,, \qquad
        \widetilde{\sigma}_c^2 = \frac{1}{BHW}\sum_{b,i,j} \left(\idx{\vc{Z}}{b,i,j,c} - \widetilde{\mu}_c\right)^2 .
    \]
    Each channel is then standardized and rescaled as before, with one pair
    $\gamma_c, \beta_c$ per channel ($2C$ parameters).

    \vspace{0.5em}
    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsblue]
        A channel plays the role of a feature: the mean is taken over the
        mini-batch \emph{and} over all spatial positions.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    In PyTorch, this is \texttt{nn.BatchNorm2d(C)} (with channels first).

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Layer normalization}
    \setlength{\parskip}{5pt}

    Tying each example to its mini-batch is costly: noisy estimates with small
    mini-batches, a poor fit for variable-length sequences or many devices, and
    different behavior in training and inference. \myalert{Layer normalization}
    (LN) computes the statistics of each example over its own features:
    \[
        \widetilde{\mu}_b = \frac{1}{D}\sum_j \idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} \,, \quad
        \widetilde{\sigma}_b^2 = \frac{1}{D}\sum_j \left(\idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} - \widetilde{\mu}_b\right)^2 , \quad
        \idx{\text{LN}(\vc{Z})}{b,j} = \gamma_j \frac{\idx{\vc{Z}}{b,j} - \widetilde{\mu}_b}{\sqrt{\widetilde{\sigma}_b^2 + \varepsilon}} + \beta_j \,.
    \]

    \begin{center}
        \small
        \begin{tabular}{lll}
               & \textbf{Statistics over}            & \textbf{Parameters} $\gamma, \beta$ \\
            \hline
            BN & each feature, across the mini-batch & one pair per feature                \\
            LN & each example, across its features   & one pair per feature                \\
        \end{tabular}
    \end{center}

    Nothing depends on other examples: the same computation in training and
    inference, even with $B=1$. LN is the standard in transformers.

    \SlideSource{Ba et al. (2016), \textit{Layer normalization}.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Layer normalization for images and for LLMs}

    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/deeper/normalization_axes.pdf}

            {\scriptsize In blue: values sharing one mean and variance.}
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}%
            \small\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}%
            \RaggedRight\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}%
            For images, LN has two common variants:

            \textbf{A}: statistics over the whole image, all $H\!\times\!W\!\times\!C$
            values (\texttt{nn.LayerNorm} learns one $\gamma, \beta$ per value).

            \textbf{B}: statistics over the $C$ channels at each position, with
            $\gamma, \beta \in \mathbb{R}^C$, as in ConvNeXt. Transformers do the
            same on each token.

            Most recent LLMs use \myalert{RMSNorm}: LN without subtracting the
            mean, $\vc{\gamma} \odot \vc{z} / \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{D}\sum_j z_j^2 + \varepsilon}$.
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \SlideSource{Wu and He (2018), \textit{Group normalization}, ECCV; Zhang and Sennrich (2019),
        \textit{Root mean square layer normalization}, NeurIPS.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why normalize across features?}
    \setlength{\parskip}{3pt}

    Standardizing each column suits inputs measured in different units. LN
    averages across the features of one example: why is this reasonable in
    hidden layers?
    \begin{itemize}\small
        \item \textbf{Hidden units are interchangeable.} They come from the same
              kind of linear map and start identically distributed (same reasoning as He initialization).
        \item \textbf{LN fixes the length of the representation}, as we see
              in the next slide.
        \item \textbf{It removes different nuisances than BN.} LN is invariant to
              rescaling the whole weight matrix and to rescaling a single input;
              BN to rescaling the weights of each unit.
    \end{itemize}

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsorange]
        This assumes units contribute in similar ways. In CNNs, units near the
        image border behave differently: there, BN still works better.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{Ba et al. (2016), \textit{Layer normalization}, Secs.~5.1 and~6.7.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What layer normalization does to a vector}

    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}%
            \small\setlength{\parskip}{5pt}%
            \RaggedRight\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}%
            Leave $\gamma, \beta$ aside. LN does two things to each vector
            $\vc{z} \in \mathbb{R}^D$:

            \textbf{1. Centering.} Subtracting the mean removes the part of
            $\vc{z}$ along $\vc{1} = (1, \ldots, 1)$. What is left, $\vc{P}\vc{z}$,
            is orthogonal to $\vc{1}$.

            \textbf{2. Scaling.} The standard deviation is
            $\sigma = \lVert\vc{P}\vc{z}\rVert / \sqrt{D}$, so dividing by it
            gives a vector of length exactly $\sqrt{D}$.

            Every example leaves LN with the \textbf{same length}, whatever its
            scale: only its direction is kept. Then $\gamma, \beta$ give each
            feature its own scale and offset back.
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.4\textwidth}
            \centering
            \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.95]
                \draw[drawblue, thick] (0,0) circle (1.4);
                \node[font=\scriptsize, text=drawblue] at (1.25,-1.35) {radius $\sqrt{D}$};
                \foreach \ang/\len in {20/0.7, 75/2.3, 140/1.0, 250/1.9} {
                    \draw[-{Stealth[length=2mm]}, drawgray] (0,0) -- (\ang:\len);
                    \draw[dashed, drawgray!60] (\ang:\len) -- (\ang:1.4);

                }

                \node[font=\scriptsize, anchor=west, text=drawblue] at (20:1.45) {$\text{LN}(\vc{z})$};
            \end{tikzpicture}

            {\scriptsize Centered vectors (gray) and their LN outputs (blue),
            in the subspace orthogonal to $\vc{1}$.}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why can we drop the mean?}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    Centering is a linear map:
    $\vc{z} - \text{mean}(\vc{z})\,\vc{1} = \vc{P}\vc{z}$, with
    $\vc{P} = \vc{I} - \frac{1}{D}\vc{1}\vc{1}^\top$. When LN follows a linear
    layer, $\vc{P}$ can be folded into it:
    \[
        \text{LN}(\vc{W}\vc{x} + \vc{b}) = \text{RMSNorm}(\vc{P}\vc{W}\vc{x} + \vc{P}\vc{b}) + \vc{\beta} \,.
    \]
    So centering adds nothing that the previous layer cannot learn. The only
    nonlinear part of LN is the division by the norm, which RMSNorm keeps. In a
    transformer, everything added to the residual stream comes out of a linear
    layer, so the same argument holds.

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        In practice, RMSNorm matches LN in accuracy and reduces running time by
        7--64\% across models.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{Zhang and Sennrich (2019), \textit{Root mean square layer normalization}, NeurIPS.}

\end{frame}

\subsection{Why normalization works}

\begin{frame}{Why does normalization help?}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    The original explanation was \textbf{internal covariate shift}: each layer
    sees inputs whose distribution keeps changing as the previous layers train,
    and BN stabilizes them.

    Later experiments do not support it: injecting noise after BN to shift the
    distributions again barely hurts training. A better-supported view is that
    BN makes the loss landscape \textbf{smoother}, so the gradient stays
    predictive over longer steps.

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsgreen]
        In practice, the most robust effect is that normalized networks train
        stably with much \textbf{larger learning rates}.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    The full picture is still debated!

    \SlideSource{Santurkar et al. (2018), \textit{How does batch normalization help optimization?}, NeurIPS;
        Bjorck et al. (2018), \textit{Understanding batch normalization}, NeurIPS;
        Lipton and Steinhardt (2018), \textit{Troubling trends in machine learning scholarship}.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Normalized layers ignore the scale of their weights}

    \begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
        \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}%
            \setlength{\parskip}{4pt}%
            \RaggedRight\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}%
            If a unit with weights $\vc{w}$ is followed by normalization,
            scaling $\vc{w}$ by any $a > 0$ changes nothing:
            \[
                \text{BN}(a\,\vc{X}\vc{w}) = \text{BN}(\vc{X}\vc{w}) \,,
                \quad \text{so} \quad \mathcal{L}(a\vc{w}) = \mathcal{L}(\vc{w}) \,.
            \]
            Only the direction of $\vc{w}$ matters: the loss is constant along
            every ray from the origin.

            Making $\vc{w}$ longer or shorter does not change the loss, so the
            gradient has no component along $\vc{w}$: it is
            \textbf{orthogonal to $\vc{w}$}, tangent to the circle of radius
            $\lVert\vc{w}\rVert$.
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.4\textwidth}
            \centering
            \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.95]
                \foreach \ang in {5,25,...,165} {
                    \draw[drawgray!35] (0,0) -- (\ang:2.8);
                }
                \draw[drawgray] (0,0) circle (2);
                \draw[-{Stealth[length=2mm]}, thick, drawblue] (0,0) -- (40:2)
                    node[midway, below right, font=\scriptsize] {$\vc{w}$};
                \draw[-{Stealth[length=2mm]}, thick, draworange] (40:2) -- ++(130:1.1)
                    node[above, font=\scriptsize] {$-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}$};
                \draw[dashed, drawgray] (0,0) -- ($(40:2) + (130:1.1)$);
                \fill (0,0) circle (1pt);
            \end{tikzpicture}

            {\scriptsize The loss is constant along each gray ray.}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \SlideSource{van Laarhoven (2017), \textit{L2 regularization versus batch and weight normalization}.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Weight decay as a learning rate}
    \setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

    Two consequences for gradient descent on these weights:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The step is orthogonal to $\vc{w}$, so it moves $\vc{w}$ off the
              circle, outwards (Pythagoras):
              $\lVert\vc{w} - \eta\nabla\mathcal{L}\rVert^2 = \lVert\vc{w}\rVert^2 + \eta^2\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}\rVert^2$.
              Plain steps can only make $\lVert\vc{w}\rVert$ grow.
        \item The gradient shrinks as $1/\lVert\vc{w}\rVert$, so the effective
              step on the direction is about $\eta / \lVert\vc{w}\rVert^2$: the
              longer $\vc{w}$, the slower its direction changes.
    \end{itemize}

    \begin{tcolorbox}[nndsorange]
        For these weights, weight decay does not regularize the function: by keeping
        $\lVert\vc{w}\rVert$ small, it sets the \textbf{effective learning rate}.
    \end{tcolorbox}

    \SlideSource{van Laarhoven (2017), \textit{L2 regularization versus batch and weight normalization}.}

\end{frame}
